Board games and puzzles have a design flaw baked in: the boxes are flimsy, they don't stack cleanly, and the second the corner crushes, small pieces start working their way out and getting lost in the couch cushions. A puzzle mid-assembly has the opposite problem — no box to go back in at all until it's finished, which can take days.

These four products handle both ends of the problem — a real shelf that keeps boxes upright instead of crushed in a pile, sturdier containers for the games worth protecting long-term, bags that actually corral loose pieces, and a way to pause and store a puzzle without losing progress.

## How to choose board game and puzzle storage that actually works

Store boxes upright on open shelving rather than stacked flat — stacking is what crushes corners and starts the slow leak of small pieces. Reserve sturdier replacement cases for the games in heaviest rotation rather than trying to upgrade the whole collection at once. And separate the "loose pieces" problem from the "in-progress puzzle" problem — they need different solutions, zipper bags for one and a roll-up mat for the other.

## FAQ

**What's the best way to stop board game pieces from going missing?**
Repackage loose components into labeled zipper bags as soon as a box starts showing wear — most missing pieces happen gradually, from a slightly torn box, not all at once.

**How do you store a puzzle you're still working on without losing your progress?**
A roll-up puzzle mat is the standard fix — it lets you roll the entire in-progress puzzle, sorted pieces and all, into a storage tube or bag between sessions without disturbing what's already been placed.

**Should board games be stored flat or upright?**
Upright, on a shelf, whenever possible — flat stacking is what crushes box corners over time, and crushed corners are usually the first step toward a box that no longer closes and pieces that start falling out.
