Chores and schedules fall apart in a household for the same basic reason: they live in someone's head instead of somewhere everyone can see. Verbal reminders get forgotten, and a schedule only one parent tracks becomes a bottleneck the moment that parent is busy or out. A visible, shared system fixes both problems at once.

These three products cover the two things a family actually needs to track: who's doing which chore, and what's happening this week.

## 1. A magnetic dry erase chore chart for multiple kids

Chores need to be visible and easy to update daily, which makes a dry erase board more practical than a printed checklist. The [magnetic dry erase chore chart](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0C53B6DT6?tag=thesortedho09-command-20) gives each kid their own row and resets weekly with a wipe, rated 4.7 stars across 2,500+ reviews, an Overall Pick in its category.

**Tip:** keep the chore list short — 3 to 5 items per kid — rather than an exhaustive list; a chart that's realistic to actually finish gets used, one that's aspirational gets ignored.

Sports practices, appointments, and events need one shared view that doesn't depend on everyone checking the same phone calendar. ## 3. A magnetic responsibility chart for younger kids

Younger kids respond better to a hands-on, visual reward system than a written checklist they can't fully read yet. ## How to choose family chore and schedule tools that actually work

Match the format to the age of the kids using it — younger kids need a hands-on, visual system with movable pieces, while older kids and teens do fine with a simple written dry erase list. Keep both the chore chart and the weekly schedule in the same physical zone, ideally somewhere in the kitchen, so checking one naturally means checking the other. And build in a regular reset — weekly for chores, at the start of each week for the schedule — since a chart that's never wiped clean stops reflecting reality and gets ignored.

## FAQ

**How many chores should actually go on a kid's chart?**
Fewer than you'd think — 3 to 5 age-appropriate tasks that can realistically be finished daily works better than a long list that becomes discouraging or gets skipped entirely.

**Where's the best place to put a family chore chart and calendar?**
The kitchen, almost always — it's the room every family member passes through multiple times a day, which makes it the one spot a shared system actually gets checked.

**Do younger kids need a different kind of chore chart than older kids?**
Yes — younger kids (roughly ages 3-9) respond better to magnetic, movable, reward-based charts, while kids old enough to read fluently do fine with a simple written dry erase list.
